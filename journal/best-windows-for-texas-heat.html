The best windows for Texas heat: what the specs actually mean

By Sebastien Pessy, owner of Sebby Windows · Updated August 2026

For Dallas–Fort Worth, the right window is a double-pane unit with a Low-E coating and argon fill in a vinyl or fiberglass frame, spec'd to an SHGC of about 0.25 or lower on west and south exposures. SHGC, not U-factor, is the number that decides whether your house is comfortable in August, and it is the number most salespeople never mention.

Why SHGC beats U-factor here

Window performance gets sold on U-factor because that is the number that matters in cold climates. U-factor measures how fast heat conducts through the whole assembly. In Minnesota, where the job is keeping heat inside for six months, it is the right number to optimize.

North Texas has the opposite problem. Our cooling load dwarfs our heating load, and the heat is not conducting through the window, it is radiating through the glass as sunlight. The number that governs that is SHGC, the solar heat gain coefficient: the fraction of the sun's energy that gets through. An SHGC of 0.60 lets sixty percent of it into your living room. An SHGC of 0.22 lets twenty-two percent in. On a west-facing wall in August that difference is the difference between a room you use and a room you avoid.

The numbers to ask for

All four appear on the NFRC label stuck to every new window. If a company will not give you the NFRC numbers for the exact unit they are quoting, that is a real answer to a different question.

Frame material, ranked for this climate

Vinyl — right for most DFW homes

Vinyl does not conduct heat, never needs painting, and a good vinyl window massively outperforms the aluminum it usually replaces. The knock on vinyl is that cheap extrusions can distort under sustained Texas sun, which is a real argument for buying a quality line rather than the cheapest one available.

Fiberglass — worth it in specific cases

Stronger, dimensionally stable, and it holds dark exterior colors far better under our sun, at a meaningful premium over comparable vinyl. Fiberglass earns that premium on very large openings and on dark-frame exteriors, where vinyl struggles.

Aluminum — the problem you are probably replacing

A huge share of DFW homes built before the mid-1990s carry single-pane aluminum. Metal conducts heat straight through the frame no matter how good the glass is. That is why those rooms are hot at 4pm regardless of the thermostat, why the west side of the house is always the worst, and why you get condensation on the frames in January. Modern thermally broken aluminum exists and performs acceptably, but it is a commercial and high-end architectural product, not what is in a 1985 tract house.

Wood — beautiful, high maintenance here

Wood interiors with clad exteriors work well and look excellent. Exposed exterior wood in North Texas sun and humidity swings is a maintenance commitment most owners underestimate.

Where to spend, if you cannot do everything

Orientation drives the whole decision. Do it in this order:

  • West. Afternoon sun at a low angle, straight through the glass, during the hottest hours. This is where low SHGC pays for itself fastest.
  • South. High sun for most of the day, large total load.
  • East. Morning heat, real but shorter and at a cooler hour.
  • North. Very little direct gain. Replace these last, and you can reasonably spec a higher SHGC here for more daylight.

What a good glass package actually saves

Be skeptical of anyone promising to cut your electric bill in half. Replacing single-pane aluminum with a good low-SHGC double-pane unit typically trims 10 to 15 percent off the cooling portion of a DFW bill. The honest reason to do it is comfort: rooms that hold temperature, an AC that cycles instead of running flat out, no radiant heat coming off the glass when you sit near it, and noticeably less street noise.

One more thing the label will not tell you

The best glass package in the world underperforms in a frame that was installed racked, unshimmed, and unsealed. Air leakage around a badly installed window routinely exceeds leakage through the assembly itself. That is why we treat the installation detail as the product, and the window as a component of it.
